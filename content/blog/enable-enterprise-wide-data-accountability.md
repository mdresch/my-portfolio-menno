In many organizations, data governance exists on paper but not in practice. There's a policy document somewhere, perhaps a data governance committee that meets occasionally — but in daily work, each department manages data its own way. Ownership is unclear, standards differ, and data quality problems bounce between teams without ever being resolved.

The cost is real. In the organization behind this business case, teams spent around **20% of their time** reconciling data discrepancies, data accuracy stood at about **65%**, and only **70% of compliance audits** were passed.

This article outlines how to **enable enterprise-wide data accountability**: a governance operating model that turns accountability for data from a vague principle into defined roles, shared policies, and measurable results across the whole organization.

## Why Decentralized Governance Fails

- **Fragmented ownership:** data is dispersed across departments with no clear accountability for its quality or use.
- **No standard policies:** each team decides its own rules for quality, retention, access, and security.
- **Persistent quality problems:** duplicates, inaccuracies, and gaps in critical data.
- **Compliance exposure:** no unified framework to demonstrate control to auditors and regulators.
- **Wasted effort:** time spent reconciling numbers instead of using them.

## The Governance Operating Model

### 1. A Governance Structure With Teeth

- **Data governance council:** senior leaders from business, IT, risk, and compliance who set priorities, approve policies, and resolve cross-functional conflicts.
- **Chief Data Officer (or equivalent):** accountable for the governance program as a whole.
- **Data owners:** senior business leaders accountable for specific data domains, such as customer, product, finance, or employee data.
- **Data stewards:** subject-matter experts who manage day-to-day quality, definitions, and issue resolution in their domain.
- **Data custodians:** IT teams who operate the systems and implement technical controls.

A clear RACI makes explicit who is responsible, accountable, consulted, and informed for each data domain and governance activity.

### 2. Federated, Not Centralized

A small central team sets standards and provides tools, while accountability for data sits with the business domains that create and use it. This balance avoids both chaos (everyone for themselves) and bottlenecks (a central team that can't keep up).

### 3. Standard Enterprise Policies

A concise policy framework covers data quality, metadata and definitions, access and security, privacy, retention, and acceptable use — written in plain language and backed by practical standards and procedures.

### 4. Tools That Make Governance Practical

A data catalog for definitions and ownership, lineage to show where data comes from, quality monitoring dashboards, and issue workflows that route problems to the responsible steward.

### 5. Measurement and Transparency

Quality scorecards per domain, compliance metrics, and issue resolution times are reported to the governance council and data owners, so accountability is visible.

### 6. Change Management

Training for owners and stewards, communication about why governance matters, and recognition for teams that improve their data. Governance succeeds when it is seen as enabling better work, not as bureaucracy.

## Choosing an Approach

| Option | Description | Indicative Investment | Trade-offs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Status quo** | Decentralized, department-by-department governance. | None upfront | Lost productivity, compliance exposure, and unreliable data |
| **2. Off-the-shelf governance platform** | A commercial governance tool with templates for policies and stewardship. | ~$300K upfront + ~$250K/year | Faster to start with vendor best practices, but limited fit and ongoing license costs |
| **3. Custom enterprise governance framework** *(recommended)* | Standard policies, defined stewardship roles, integrated tools, and change management, tailored to the organization. | ~$500K upfront + ~$100K/year | 12-18 months and dedicated effort, but a model that fits how the organization works |

Whichever option is chosen, the operating model — roles, policies, and decision rights — matters more than the tool. Tools support accountability; they can't create it.

## Managing the Risks

| Risk | Probability | Impact | Mitigation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Low stakeholder adoption | High | High | Change management, training, communication, and incentives |
| Scope creep | Medium | High | Clear scope boundaries and a change control board |
| Resource constraints | Medium | Medium | Executive sponsorship and protected time for stewards |
| Technology integration issues | Medium | Medium | Proof-of-concept testing with existing systems |
| Regulatory changes | Low | High | An adaptable framework and compliance monitoring |

Start where the pain is greatest — usually customer, product, or financial data — and show results there before expanding to other domains.

## Measuring Success

| Compliance audit pass rate | 70% | 95% |
| Time to produce a report | 4 hours | 2 hours |
| Adoption of governance roles and processes | — | 85% |
| Critical data domains with a named owner and steward | — | 100% |
| Annual new revenue enabled by better data | $0 | $500K |

## Conclusion

Data accountability can't be the responsibility of a single team, and it can't be left to chance. It needs an operating model: a council that sets direction, owners and stewards who are accountable for their domains, policies everyone follows, tools that make governance practical, and metrics that show whether it's working.

When those pieces are in place, data stops being nobody's job and becomes everybody's asset — reliable enough to run the business on, and trustworthy enough to satisfy auditors, regulators, and customers.
