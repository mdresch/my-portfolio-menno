Every organization has them: approvals that sit in someone's inbox for days, data retyped from one system into another, month-end reports assembled by hand from five spreadsheets, and processes that exist only as "tribal knowledge" in the heads of a few experienced people.

The temptation is to throw technology at the problem — buy an RPA platform, build some bots, and declare victory. But automating a broken process just gives you a faster broken process. The approach in this article starts from a different principle: **optimize first, then automate.**

It outlines a **Process Optimization & Automation** initiative I designed to remove operational drag, embed intelligent automation into core workflows, and build a culture of continuous improvement.

## The Pain of Inefficient Processes

Inefficient processes hurt everyone, just in different ways:

- **Frontline employees** spend their days on repetitive data entry, jumping between disconnected systems and inventing workarounds — a recipe for burnout and errors.
- **Team managers** lack visibility into bottlenecks, struggle to forecast capacity, and spend their time firefighting exceptions instead of coaching.
- **Executives** make decisions on outdated or incomplete data and watch operational costs eat into margins.
- **Customers** feel it downstream as slower service, inconsistent communication, and errors in orders or invoices.

In the organization behind this business case, the symptoms were concrete:

- A **45-day average approval cycle**, against a benchmark of around 10 days.
- A **22% error rate** in financial workflows such as invoice processing.
- About **60% of tasks** requiring manual intervention.
- Legacy systems — including an older SAP ECC environment — with no APIs and no automation in place.
- Low employee engagement, with an **eNPS of 32**.

### The Cost of Inaction

| Impact Area | Annual Cost |
| --- | --- |
| Productivity loss (the equivalent of 12 FTEs on rework) | $1,800,000 |
| Opportunity cost from slow approvals | $1,200,000 |
| Error-related costs in accounts payable and receivable | $950,000 |
| Compliance risk (SOX) | $500,000 |
| Employee turnover | $420,000 |
| **Total** | **$4,870,000** |

Doing nothing isn't neutral. It is a decision to accept these losses every year — plus a growing pile of "operational debt" that becomes harder and more expensive to fix the longer it is left.

## The Approach: Four Phases

### 1. Discover & Design — Understand Before You Optimize

Work starts with mapping how work *actually* gets done. Using techniques such as **Value Stream Mapping**, every touchpoint, system, handoff, and delay in a process is made visible. Root cause analysis — for example, a fishbone analysis across people, process, technology, data, and external factors — separates real causes from symptoms. The phase ends with a future-state process design, built together with the people who do the work.

### 2. Streamline & Simplify — Lean Before Bots

Before anything is automated, waste is removed. Applying **Lean** principles, the team eliminates unnecessary handoffs, redundant approvals, and over-processing — the classic "eight wastes." **Kaizen** events solve specific problems quickly and give teams ownership of their own workflows.

### 3. Automate & Integrate — Where It Pays Off

Only now do we automate. Candidate processes are scored and prioritized using ROI modeling, and the right platform is selected. **RPA bots** take over repetitive, rules-based tasks, while **AI/ML** handles more complex work such as dynamic approval routing and exception handling. The goal is end-to-end automation from intake to outcome — not isolated task bots.

### 4. Adopt & Sustain — The Human Side

This phase runs alongside all the others. **Organizational change management (OCM)** builds alignment through transparent communication, training, and stakeholder engagement. After go-live, benefit-tracking dashboards and an ROI model show whether the promised value is actually being delivered — and feed the roadmap for what to improve next.

## What Makes This Approach Different

Many automation initiatives fail because they focus only on deploying bots. This one is built on three commitments:

1. **Optimize first.** We refuse to automate a bad process.
2. **Integrate people, process, and technology.** Lasting change only happens when all three are addressed together.
3. **Focus on value realization.** The work isn't finished when a bot goes live; it's finished when the promised business value has been measured and realized.

## Start With a Quick Win

The best way to build momentum is a focused **4-6 week pilot** on a process that is high-volume, rules-based, and stable — invoice processing in Accounts Payable is a classic candidate. A structured scorecard weighs value against complexity, so the pilot is chosen on evidence rather than enthusiasm. Picking the wrong pilot — something complex, unstable, or low-value — is one of the fastest ways to lose executive confidence.

The pilot phase is budgeted at around **€253,000**:

A successful pilot then becomes the proof point for a full-scale rollout, funded on the basis of measured results.

## The Business Case for the Full Program

The business case compared three options for the broader program:

| Option | Description | Assessment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Status quo** | Keep manual processes, with minor training and tool upgrades. | No upfront cost, but $4.87M a year in inefficiency persists. |
| **2. Off-the-shelf RPA** | Deploy a commercial RPA platform for high-volume tasks. | $500K upfront and fast to deploy, but limited to rules-based tasks (~40% automation coverage) and around $1.2M a year in licensing. |
| **3. End-to-end optimization & automation** *(recommended)* | Lean Six Sigma redesign, RPA plus AI/ML for complex workflows, and OCM to drive adoption. | $1.5M upfront and ~$600K a year, with ~80% automation coverage and an architecture ready for future AI. |

| --- | --- | --- | --- |

## Managing the Risks

| Risk | Mitigation |
| --- | --- |
| **Employee resistance** from fear of job loss | A transparent OCM plan that frames automation as *augmenting* people, not replacing them; involve employees in design; "Automation Champions" programs |
| **Waning executive sponsorship** | A formal steering committee and monthly reporting on value delivered |
| **Poor pilot selection** | A structured scoring framework (volume, stability, rules-based) and joint business-IT agreement |
| **Legacy systems without APIs** | Technical proofs of concept during discovery; UI automation as a fallback; budget for complexity |
| **Benefits falling short** | Baseline metrics before starting, benefit-tracking dashboards, regular value audits, and conservative expectations |
| **Scope creep in redesign** | Agile delivery in fixed two-week iterations |

## Conclusion

Automation is not a technology project; it is a business transformation that uses technology. The organizations that get lasting value from it do three things differently: they understand their processes before changing them, they simplify before they automate, and they invest as much in people and adoption as in bots.

Start with one well-chosen process. Measure everything. Prove the value. Then scale — and turn processes from a source of drag into an engine for growth.
