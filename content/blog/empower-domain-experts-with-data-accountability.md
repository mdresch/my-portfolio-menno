Ask who owns the definition of "customer" in most organizations and you'll get several different answers — or a shrug. Sales counts anyone with an open opportunity, finance counts anyone who has been invoiced, and marketing counts anyone on the mailing list. Each team's reports are internally consistent and mutually contradictory.

The result is familiar: leadership asks for three versions of the same report to see which one is right, analysts spend their days reconciling numbers, and trust in data quietly erodes.

These problems are rarely technical at heart. They're **ownership** problems. This article outlines how to **empower domain experts with data accountability**: shifting ownership of data to the people who understand it best, and giving them the definitions, tools, and incentives to take that responsibility seriously.

## Why Data Goes Wrong

- **Fragmented ownership:** nobody is clearly responsible for the quality, definition, or use of key data. Domain experts *use* the data but have neither the authority nor the tools to fix it.
- **IT as a bottleneck:** central IT or data teams act as intermediaries for every correction, creating queues and delays — even though they often understand the business meaning least.
- **Inconsistent definitions:** key terms mean different things in different departments, with no central glossary to settle the matter.
- **Late discovery:** data issues are found after they've already affected decisions or operations.
- **No incentives:** data quality isn't part of anyone's goals, so it's always somebody else's problem.
- **No feedback loop** between the people who produce data and the people who consume it.

In the organization behind this business case, domain experts spent around **15% of their time** validating and reconciling data, decisions took an average of **five days** waiting for reliable numbers, and only **60%** of stakeholders said they trusted the data.

## The Approach: Accountability Where the Knowledge Is

### 1. Clear Roles

Every critical data domain — customers, products, suppliers, finance, employees — gets a named **data owner** from the business and one or more **data stewards** who handle day-to-day quality. IT and data teams shift from gatekeepers to enablers, providing platforms, automation, and expertise.

### 2. Shared Definitions

A **business glossary** and central repository define key terms, calculation rules, and valid values — agreed by the owners and visible to everyone. When "customer" means one thing everywhere, reports start to agree.

### 3. Transparency

**Data lineage** shows where data comes from and how it's transformed, so errors can be traced to their source rather than argued about in meetings.

### 4. Automated Quality Workflows

Validation rules catch issues as data enters systems. When something goes wrong, an issue workflow routes it straight to the responsible steward, with clear resolution times — instead of an email chain through IT.

### 5. Measurement and Incentives

Data quality scorecards per domain make quality visible, and data outcomes become part of owners' and stewards' objectives. What gets measured — and recognized — gets done.

### 6. Training and Change Management

Owners and stewards are trained in their new roles, and the change is communicated as empowerment rather than extra work: less time firefighting, more control over the data they depend on.

## Choosing an Approach

| Option | Description | Indicative Cost | Trade-offs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Status quo** | Keep decentralized, ambiguous ownership. | None upfront | Ongoing lost productivity, slow decisions, and compliance exposure |
| **2. Off-the-shelf governance tool** | Deploy a commercial data governance platform for definitions, lineage, and ownership. | ~$220K upfront + ~$80K/year | Faster (6-9 months), but limited fit with specific requirements and harder adoption if imposed as a tool |
| **3. Custom ownership framework with integrated governance tools** *(recommended)* | A tailored framework of roles, definitions, and workflows, supported by integrated governance tooling. | ~$450K upfront + ~$50K/year | Longer (12-18 months), but aligned with how the organization actually works — and therefore more likely to stick |

The key lesson is that tools don't create accountability. A governance platform is only effective when it supports clearly defined roles and processes the business has agreed to.

## Implementation Roadmap

| Phase | Duration | Key Activities |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Planning** | 2 months | Charter, scope, stakeholder analysis, budget |
| **2. Design** | 3 months | Ownership framework, tool selection and configuration, training materials |
| **3. Development** | 6 months | Tool customization, system integration, automated workflows |
| **4. Testing** | 2 months | Pilots in selected domains; validate quality and tool performance |
| **5. Deployment** | 3 months | Rollout across domains; training programs; adoption monitoring |
| **6. Optimization** | 2 months | Refine the framework and workflows based on feedback |

Starting with one or two high-impact domains — often customer and product data — builds proof and momentum before the model is rolled out more widely.

## Managing the Risks

| Risk | Probability | Impact | Mitigation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Resistance to change | High | High | Change management, engagement, training, and incentives |
| Low adoption by domain experts | High | High | User-friendly tools and ongoing support |
| Existing data quality issues | High | High | Baseline metrics and automated validation from the start |
| Resource constraints | Medium | High | Executive sponsorship to protect time for owners and stewards |
| Tool integration challenges | Medium | High | Technical assessment before tool selection |
| Scope creep | Medium | Medium | Formal change control |

## Conclusion

Data quality can't be delegated to IT. The people who understand what the data means — and who suffer most when it's wrong — are the ones best placed to own it. When those domain experts have clear roles, shared definitions, transparent lineage, and workflows that make fixing data easy, quality stops being an afterthought.

The payoff goes well beyond cleaner reports: faster decisions, less time wasted on reconciliation, and an organization that actually trusts the numbers it runs on.
